    Reading these numbers

    • These are micro-benchmarks of the cell alone, run on shared CI machines. Compare values within one machine and one run.
    • The subjects do not offer the same guarantees. Some hand out borrows and others owned Arcs; some return old values to the writer and others drop them on a reader thread; some let a slow reader block the writer. COMPARISONS.md describes the differences.
    • The writer in “Read beside a writer” publishes continuously, and the readers in “Write” read continuously. Both are far harsher than a typical read-mostly workload.
    • In “Read beside a writer” a subject with cheap writes gets more of them, and its readers pay for each one, so its reads can look slower than those of a subject with a slow writer. The operation named after a number of writes per second holds the writer of every subject to that rate. Every read beside a writer shows the writes per second there really were; † marks a writer that was too slow for the rate, so its readers saw fewer writes than the others.
    • The CI machines have 4 CPUs. Reads without a writer go up to 4 readers; with a writer the sweep stops at 3 so the writer has a CPU of its own. “Read beside a writer” and “Write” are measured once more with 8 readers, twice the CPUs: the threads then take turns on the CPUs, and a reader or the writer can lose its CPU in the middle of a read or a write. A read is timed by the clock, so it takes longer there for every subject; what differs is how much a thread that is not running holds up the others. A subject can stall altogether there: if its writer (or its readers) cannot get 1,000 operations done in 2 seconds, it is not measured and is listed as stalled.
    • Where no single read or write was timed (a run with that turned off), the range drawn around each dot on the log scale is criterion's 95% confidence interval for the median.
    • The median says nothing about the slow reads and writes. So every read (or write) of every operation was also timed on its own for half a second. On the log scale each row then runs from the median to the time that 99.9% of them stayed within, with a tick at 99%, and on in dots to the slowest one. “Compare by” picks which of those the rows are sorted by and compared with local-rcu by, and which one the chart of the reader counts shows. Each of those times includes one reading of the clock, a few tens of nanoseconds, and the shaded part of the chart is where that puts them out of reach: for the fastest subjects they say more about the clock than about the read, and what they are for is the stalls of microseconds and more. The pale shape in a row is where the timings are, as high as there are many of them at that time, and the chart below the ranked one has all of them for local-rcu and for the row you click. The slowest one is a single read or write of a short run on a shared machine, and mostly a matter of which thread lost its CPU.
    • ◆ and ◇ mark subjects whose readers keep old values alive while idle. It is measured, not declared: the writer publishes a value, one reader reads it and then does nothing, and the writer publishes 100,000 more. Two things are counted afterwards: the allocations made by those writes that are still alive, and the old values that can still be found in memory the subject holds (freed memory is wiped). The marks go by the allocations; a subject may need more than one per value it keeps, so the two counts differ. Each measurement runs in a process of its own, so that a subject with a process-wide collector starts empty. ◆: most of those are still allocated afterwards, and would not be had the reader gone away first. ◇: only the value the reader read is still allocated, typically because the reader holds an Arc of it. local-rcu-cached is ◆ by design (call release() before going idle); plain local-rcu keeps nothing.
    • ≡ marks subjects that free old values in batches. Also measured: the writer publishes 100,000 values with no reader anywhere and then stops. A subject is marked when more than a handful of allocations are still alive (at least 8, well short of all of them): they wait for a later write, which may never come. The count is taken again 200 ms later; a subject whose leftovers are gone by then would be marked ↻ instead, for a background task doing the freeing. No subject here has one. basedrop's collector could run on another thread, but this benchmark runs it in the writer after every write.
    • Copy marks subjects that can only hold Copy values (plain data with no pointers, nothing that owns memory). A read copies the whole value out instead of borrowing it. That is fast for the four u64s used here, costs more the larger the value, and rules out a Vec, a String or a map.
    • baseline-leak never frees anything. It is the floor, not a contender.
